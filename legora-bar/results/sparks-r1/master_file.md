# 2. Introduction and Regulatory Context

## 2.1 Background

This Master File sets out the global transfer pricing arrangements of Freshworks Inc. (together with its consolidated subsidiaries, "the Group") in respect of the financial year ended 31 December 2023 ("FY2023"). It forms one component of a two-tier documentation structure:

- This Master File provides a consolidated view of the Group's worldwide operations, intercompany arrangements, and transfer pricing policies.
- Accompanying Local Files contain entity-level transactional analyses demonstrating arm's length compliance in each jurisdiction.

Freshworks is a pure-play software-as-a-service ("SaaS") business. It builds and sells AI-powered business software across two product families — customer experience ("CX") and IT service management ("ITSM") — and generates approximately 97.7% of its revenue from subscriptions, the remainder from professional services. Because the Group neither manufactures nor distributes tangible goods, its transfer pricing profile is driven almost entirely by the location of software development, the ownership of intellectual property, and the location of customer-facing sales and marketing activity. Those three factors define the intercompany transaction population described in Sections 4, 5 and 6.

In FY2023 the Group recorded consolidated revenue of US$596.4 million (FY2022: US$498.0 million; FY2021: US$371.0 million) and an operating loss of US$170.2 million. The Group employed approximately 4,900 people as at 31 December 2023, of whom approximately 4,100 (84%) were based in India. All intellectual property of the Group is legally owned by Freshworks Inc. in the United States, and substantially all core software development is performed by Freshworks Technologies Private Limited in India under a contract R&D arrangement.

The intercompany transactions documented in this Master File for FY2023 comprise, in principal part, the quarterly cost-plus services charges invoiced by six subsidiaries to Freshworks Inc., totalling US$319.2 million in FY2023. Section 10 identifies the additional transaction categories that the Group's functional interviews indicate exist but that are not captured in the Group's intercompany transaction ledger, and which are therefore only partially documented here.

## 3.2 History and Evolution

Freshworks was founded in Chennai, India in 2010, initially as a provider of a single helpdesk product, Freshdesk. The company's founding premise was to offer small and mid-sized businesses enterprise-grade customer support software at a price point and level of usability that incumbent vendors did not serve. That premise shaped two structural features that persist today and that are central to the Group's transfer pricing profile: an engineering organisation built in India, and a product-led, low-touch sales model capable of serving customers globally from a small number of commercial hubs.

The product portfolio expanded in stages. Freshservice followed Freshdesk as an ITSM product, extending the Group from customer-facing support into internal IT operations. Freshsales and Freshmarketer added sales-force and marketing automation, and Freshchat added conversational messaging. Together these products formed the two solution families — customer experience and IT service management — through which the Group now goes to market. The most recent addition to the portfolio is Freddy AI, a proprietary artificial intelligence engine embedded across all products, which is developed and maintained by the Group's Indian engineering organisation under the direction of the US product organisation.

Freshworks relocated its headquarters to San Mateo, California, and in September 2021 completed its initial public offering on NASDAQ, raising capital that left the Group debt-free and with a substantial cash and investment balance. The Group expanded into Europe through subsidiaries in the United Kingdom, Germany, France and the Netherlands, and into Asia-Pacific through entities in Singapore and Australia; Natero Ireland Limited came into the Group through the acquisition of Natero, a customer success analytics business. In June 2024 — that is, after the FY2023 period covered by this Master File — the Group acquired Device42, an IT asset management business, for approximately US$238 million, adding D42 Parent, Inc. and Device42, Inc. as US subsidiaries.

Two further developments after the reporting period are relevant context for the FY2023 position. In November 2024 the Group announced a restructuring plan to realign its workforce around AI priorities, incurring approximately US$9.7 million in severance charges. And throughout FY2024 the Group continued to grow revenue (to approximately US$720 million) while reducing its operating loss — evidence that the scale-up phase described above is maturing, but not a change to the operating model described in Sections 4 to 6.

The Group's legal entity structure has been materially stable across FY2021 to FY2023. The principal changes in that window were intra-group mergers in India (the absorption of Osmnez Technologies Private Limited and Sassist IO India Private Limited into Freshworks Technologies Private Limited, effective after the Indian financial year ended 31 March 2023, and of Framebench Technologies Private Limited thereafter), neither of which altered the functional role of the Indian entity.

### 3.3.3 Governance Observations Relevant to Transfer Pricing

Three features of the Group's governance bear directly on the functional analysis and on the reliability of the Group's allocation of risk and profit:

1. **Centralised decision-making.** All managing directors report to the Group CEO in the United States, and the Group's functional heads (CTO, CPO, CRO) direct their respective functions globally rather than through the local entities. Strategic decisions affecting product roadmap, pricing, market entry, and headcount are taken in the United States.
2. **Common directorships.** Pamela Anne Sergeeff is a director of FW-UK, FW-DE (as managing director signatory), FW-NL, and Natero IE, and signs the statutory accounts of those entities. This is consistent with the Parent exercising control over subsidiary decision-making, and it supports the characterisation of the subsidiaries as limited-risk service providers rather than entrepreneurial entities.
3. **No local boards with independent authority.** None of the subsidiaries holds, develops, or controls intellectual property, and none bears market or product-development risk in the sense contemplated by Chapter VI of the OECD Guidelines. The commercial risk of the Group's SaaS business — customer churn, pricing, competitive displacement, product failure — is borne by Freshworks Inc., which is the legal owner of all Group IP and the contracting party with the Group's largest customers.

These observations are consistent with the transfer pricing policies in Section 6 and are the reason the Group's subsidiaries are remunerated on a cost-plus basis rather than on a profit-split or residual-profit basis.

# 4. Business Model and Value Chain

## 4.1 Industry Context

The Group operates in the enterprise software market, specifically in the software-as-a-service segment. Two structural features of that market shape the Group's transfer pricing profile.

First, the economics of SaaS are dominated by the cost of developing and maintaining the software and the cost of acquiring and retaining customers, rather than by the cost of delivering each unit of output. Once software is built and hosted, the marginal cost of serving an additional customer is close to zero. This is reflected in the Group's consolidated gross margin of 82.7% in FY2023 (FY2022: 80.8%; FY2021: 79.0%) — the cost of revenue line consists principally of cloud infrastructure, customer support salaries, and amortisation of capitalised software, not of goods. The consequence for transfer pricing is that the value of the business resides in intangibles (software code, brand, customer relationships, data) rather than in tangible assets or inventory, and that the correct question in an intangibles analysis is where the functions that create and sustain those intangibles are performed.

Second, the market is characterised by high customer acquisition costs and subscription-based recurring revenue. SaaS vendors typically spend more on sales and marketing than on research and development in the growth phase, and the Group is no exception: in FY2023 sales and marketing expense of US$357.8 million was 2.6 times research and development expense of US$137.8 million. This asymmetry means that the sales and marketing subsidiaries in Europe — which are remunerated on a cost-plus basis — carry a very large cost base relative to their headcount, and that any shift in the mark-up applied to that base has a material profit effect in the European jurisdictions.

The competitive landscape consists of large diversified enterprise software vendors and specialist point solutions. The Group competes principally on ease of use, time to value, and total cost of ownership for mid-market and enterprise customers, and its go-to-market model is deliberately product-led: customers can adopt products through self-service and expand usage organically. Net dollar retention was 108% in FY2023. The Group's pricing is set centrally in the United States; no subsidiary has pricing authority.

Freshworks does not manufacture, warehouse, or ship physical products. There are no intercompany transactions in tangible goods, no consignment stock arrangements, and no contract manufacturing or contract distribution arrangements in the FY2023 perimeter.

### 4.2.1 Revenue Model

The Group sells subscriptions directly to customers and indirectly through channel partners under non-cancellable, non-refundable arrangements. Subscription revenue is recognised ratably over the contract term, and reseller revenue is recorded net of reseller discounts. Approximately 90% of annual recurring revenue comes from customers paying more than US$5,000 per year.

**Which entity is the contracting party?** This question is central to the functional analysis, and the documentation is internally inconsistent on it:

- The audited statutory accounts of every subsidiary show revenue consisting exclusively of cost-plus recharges from Freshworks Inc. FW-UK and FW-DE state that they "provide sales and marketing services to their parent company" and that "the parent company is the operating company"; FW-FR states that it remunerates itself "par un système de mark-up sur ses charges d'exploitation". On this evidence, no subsidiary earns third-party revenue and no subsidiary is the contracting party of record.
- The Group's functional analysis interview, however, states that "customer contracts are held locally by each entity", that the UK team "negotiate[s] contracts", and that the local entities bear "some credit risk on their local customer receivables".

These two propositions cannot both be true in the form stated. The distinction matters because an entity that holds customer contracts and bears credit risk on them may own a customer-relationship intangible and may bear market risk — in which case a pure cost-plus remuneration would be inappropriate and the "limited-risk distributor" language used by management would need to be reflected in the pricing. Section 10, item 4 records this as a priority item for resolution. Until it is resolved, this Master File describes the subsidiary arrangements as cost-plus service arrangements, which is what the audited accounts evidence.

### 4.2.2 Products

The portfolio comprises two solution families:

**Customer Experience (CX)** — Freshdesk (helpdesk and ticketing), Freshchat (messaging and conversational support), Freshsales (sales force automation), Freshmarketer (marketing automation), and Freshcaller (cloud telephony). These products serve customer support, sales, and marketing teams.

**IT Service Management (ITSM)** — Freshservice (IT service management, including incident, problem, change, and asset management), together with its project management and orchestration modules.

**Platform and AI** — Freddy AI, a proprietary artificial intelligence engine embedded across all products (including Freddy Copilot and Freddy Insights), and the Freshworks Neo platform on which all products run. Freddy AI and the Neo platform are developed by the Group's Indian engineering organisation and are not licensed or sold separately.

### 4.2.3 Key Value Drivers

The Group's management identifies the following as the principal drivers of value in its business:

1. **Product development and engineering capability** — the ability to design, build, and ship software at pace, concentrated in India under the direction of the US product organisation. More than 2,000 engineers are based in India.
2. **Intellectual property** — the source code, architecture, data models, AI models, patents, trademarks, and documentation comprising the products, all legally owned by Freshworks Inc.
3. **Brand and demand generation** — marketing spend, brand equity, and the product-led growth engine that drives inbound adoption.
4. **Customer acquisition and retention** — outbound sales capability (particularly for mid-market and enterprise), customer success, and the resulting installed base and recurring revenue. Net dollar retention was 108% in FY2023.
5. **Cloud infrastructure and reliability** — the AWS-hosted infrastructure across US, EU, India, and Australia regions, and the engineering discipline required to keep the platform available.
6. **Data** — telemetry, usage data, and customer interaction data used to train AI models and improve products.

Each of these value drivers is controlled by Freshworks Inc. The subsidiaries contribute to them: FW-IN performs the engineering; the European entities perform the sales and marketing; FW-NL and Natero IE perform support engineering and data science. But none of the subsidiaries owns a value driver in the sense of controlling the decisions that determine how it is exploited, and none bears the residual profit or loss from it.

### 4.3.1 Procurement and Inbound Logistics

Freshworks has no physical supply chain. There are no raw materials, no components, no inbound logistics, and no intercompany transactions in goods. "Procurement" in this business means the acquisition of the inputs required to build and operate software, and it is centrally controlled.

**Cloud infrastructure.** All production infrastructure is contracted by Freshworks Inc. directly with third-party providers, principally Amazon Web Services, across regions in the United States, the EU, India, and Australia. FW-US bears the cost; it appears in the Group's consolidated cost of revenue and is not recharged to any subsidiary. Data residency requirements (GDPR, UK GDPR, Australia's Privacy Act, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023) have driven the regional footprint but have not led to any subsidiary owning or contracting infrastructure in its own name.

**Third-party software and development tools.** Licences for development tooling, security software, monitoring, and third-party SaaS applications are procured centrally by FW-US and by FW-IN under the Parent's procurement policy. No intra-group licence of third-party software occurs.

**Development hardware and office infrastructure.** Equipment, leasehold improvements, and office fit-out are procured locally by each entity for its own use. The Group held total long-lived assets of US$55.5 million at 31 December 2023, of which US$30.6 million was in Asia Pacific (primarily India), US$22.6 million in North America, and US$2.2 million in EMEA. These assets are not transferred or leased between entities.

**Professional services.** Audit, tax, legal, transfer pricing, and consulting services are procured centrally by FW-US or locally by the entity that requires them; no material intercompany recharge of third-party professional fees occurs.

**Implication for transfer pricing.** Because procurement is centralised at FW-US and no goods or third-party services flow between entities, the Group has no tangible-goods transactions and no "pass-through" cost recharges of significance. The transfer pricing population is therefore unusually simple: it consists almost entirely of service fees (Section 4.6). The one procurement item that matters for transfer pricing is the AWS cost, because it is a large third-party cost borne by the Parent and excluded from every subsidiary's cost base — which means the subsidiaries are not remunerated for, and do not share in, the economies of scale the Group achieves on infrastructure.

### 4.3.2 Production and Operations

"Production" for a SaaS business means building the software and delivering it as a live service. Both halves of that activity are described below.

**Product strategy and definition (FW-US).** Product management sits in the United States. The Chief Product Officer, working with the CEO and CTO, defines the roadmap, prioritises features, specifies requirements, and approves releases. As the CTO explains, "the what and the why come from the US leadership team"; the engineering organisation retains autonomy on how to implement.

**Engineering execution (FW-IN).** Freshworks Technologies Private Limited performs the bulk of engineering under the direction of the Group CTO and the US product organisation. Its engineering organisation is led by Rajesh Balaji (Managing Director, India) with VPs for Engineering and Product Development and directors for Quality Assurance & Testing and IT & Infrastructure. Functions include coding, architecture implementation, testing, release engineering, documentation, and AI model development and evaluation. More than 2,000 engineers are based in India, described in the Form 10-K as "a majority of our software engineering resources" and a strategic advantage.

**Specialist development (Natero IE).** Natero Ireland Limited performs engineering, data science, and platform integration services with a headcount of two, remunerated in FY2023 at cost plus 5.00%.

**Maintenance, testing and QA support (FW-NL).** Freshworks Technologies B.V. provides "software maintenance, testing services and quality assurance support" alongside back-office IT support, consultancy and advisory support, documentation, and customer and market research support, remunerated at cost plus 7.50%.

**Hosting and delivery (FW-US).** Products are delivered exclusively as cloud services; subscription arrangements "do not provide customers with the right to take possession of the software supporting the solutions" and are accounted for as service arrangements. Platform operations, security, reliability engineering, release management, and service-level accountability sit with FW-US. The Group records no intercompany transaction for hosting or delivery.

**Intellectual property ownership (FW-US).** All IP arising from these activities is legally owned by Freshworks Inc. (Section 5).

Two features of this arrangement require attention. First, the engineering performed in India is not routine: it includes core platform development and Freddy AI model development, functions that in many software groups are performed by the IP-owning entity. Second, FW-IN's contracts are described in its statutory filings as "contract R&D services" and are benchmarked as such, which presupposes that FW-IN bears no development risk and does not share in the upside from successful products. Section 6.4 examines whether the 15.00% mark-up applied to that cost base is consistent with either characterisation.

### 4.3.3 Marketing, Sales, and Distribution

The Group's go-to-market model combines a product-led, self-service motion with an outbound enterprise motion. Demand generation, brand, pricing, and the product-led funnel are managed globally from the United States; regional field sales is performed by local subsidiaries.

**Central functions (FW-US).** Marketing strategy, demand generation, brand management, website and digital funnel, pricing, discounting policy, and commercial policy are owned by FW-US. List prices are set centrally by the US product and finance teams. The CRO and VP Global Sales sit in the United States, as does the marketing leadership. Management confirms that "all decisions around pricing, market entry, partnerships, and M&A" are made in the United States.

**Regional field sales (FW-UK, FW-DE, FW-FR, FW-NL, FW-AU, FW-SG).** FW-UK covers EMEA North (led by a Head of Sales, EMEA North, with customer success and pre-sales); FW-DE covers DACH; FW-FR covers France; FW-NL covers Benelux; FW-AU covers ANZ; FW-SG covers South-East Asia and partnerships. These entities prospect, qualify, demonstrate, negotiate, and manage pipeline, and run local campaigns, events, webinars, and digital advertising. They follow a global playbook and use centrally developed marketing materials adapted locally (for example, translated into German for DACH). Local representatives may discount within pre-approved thresholds of typically 15–20% on standard deals; beyond that requires regional or central approval, and local teams cannot create pricing tiers or change the pricing structure.

**Distribution channels.** Subscriptions are sold directly and through channel partners — resellers and systems integrators — with pricing to resellers discounted to allow the reseller a margin, and reseller revenue recorded net of reseller discounts. Channel agreements are entered into centrally; no subsidiary is a principal in a reseller arrangement.

**Remuneration.** The European sales entities are remunerated by FW-US on a cost-plus basis. Their statutory accounts state: "The Company provides sales and marketing services to its parent company, and the revenue is recognized on a cost-plus basis. The parent company is the operating company and the Company is a regional entity." FY2023 mark-ups were 7.07% (FW-UK), 7.86% (FW-DE), 8.00% (FW-FR) and 7.50% (FW-NL).

**Characterisation conflict.** Management describes the European entities as "limited-risk distributors" earning "a few percentage points on sales". That language is inconsistent with the documentation and with the pricing applied. A distributor earns a margin on sales revenue it recognises; these entities recognise no third-party sales revenue, earn a mark-up on costs, and are benchmarked on full cost mark-up rather than on sales. The Group should adopt a single characterisation — limited-risk service provider (consistent with the accounts and the benchmark studies) or limited-risk distributor (consistent with management's description, but requiring a resale price method or a sales-based margin and different comparables). See Section 10, item 4.

**An important gap.** Neither FW-AU nor FW-SG appears in the Group's intercompany ledger for FY2021–FY2023, although both are regional sales entities and both are named tested parties in the Sales & Marketing Services (Asia-Pacific) study. Either APAC remuneration is processed outside the ledger, or the APAC entities operate unrecompensed. An unrecompensed sales function in Australia and Singapore would be a substantial and unsubstantiated profit allocation away from those jurisdictions.

### 4.3.4 After-Sales and Support

Support and professional services sit at the delivery end of the value chain and scale with the installed base rather than with product scope.

**Technical support (FW-IN).** The Group's customer support organisation is based predominantly in India and is part of FW-IN's service scope. Support is tiered, from first-line triage to technical escalation, and delivered remotely across time zones; "our support teams handle tickets from customers worldwide from Chennai and Bangalore". Support salaries form part of FW-IN's cost base and are remunerated at cost plus 15.00% in FY2023.

**Customer success.** Customer success management is performed locally in the larger markets (FW-UK, FW-AU, FW-SG) and centrally in the United States. It is part of the local sales services scope rather than a separately charged service.

**Inside sales (FW-IN).** The Group's in-bound and inside sales function is based in India; the Form 10-K discloses that Indian operations include "engineering, product design, customer support, in-bound sales and general and administrative personnel". Inside sales is functionally part of the sales and marketing value chain but is performed by FW-IN and remunerated within the FW-IN cost-plus arrangement rather than under a separate sales services agreement.

**Professional services (FW-US and FW-IN).** The professional services organisation — product configuration, data migration, systems integration, and training — accounts for less than 5% of Group revenue and is managed within the US organisation, staffed partly from India. No separate intercompany professional services agreement is recorded in the ledger; the activity is subsumed within the FW-IN arrangement.

**No warranty or returns.** Because the Group sells subscriptions to a cloud service, there is no warranty repair, no returns processing, and no after-sales spare-parts activity. Service credits under service-level commitments are a commercial adjustment to revenue, not a transfer pricing transaction.

**Implication for the functional analysis.** Because the Indian entity performs engineering, support, and inside sales, its cost base aggregates functions that the Group's benchmark studies treat separately. FW-IN is benchmarked as a contract software developer and as a provider of management services, but its cost base also includes support and inside-sales personnel. A mark-up applied uniformly across a mixed cost base is not, strictly, a benchmarked result for any single function. This is a methodological weakness addressed at Section 6.4 and Section 10, item 2.

### 4.6.2 Contractual Documentation

Each subsidiary's statutory accounts refer to an "Intercompany Service Agreement" with Freshworks Inc. governing the cost-plus arrangement (for example, FW-DE's management report refers to "dem bestehenden Intercompany Service Agreements", and FW-UK's accounts describe services invoiced "based on a 'cost-plus' model"). ### 4.6.3 Service Characterisation

All six recorded arrangements are intra-group services within the meaning of Chapter VII of the OECD Guidelines. Applying the Chapter VII framework:

- **Benefit test.** Each service is one that an independent enterprise in the recipient's position would be willing to pay for, or would perform for itself. Software development, sales, support, and back-office functions are all core to a SaaS business.
- **Shareholder activity exclusion.** None of the recorded charges appears to include shareholder costs (costs of a parent's own legal structure, group reporting, listing compliance, or investor relations). The Group should confirm this, because FW-US retains significant costs — SEC reporting, investor relations, board costs, group audit — which must *not* be charged out to subsidiaries.
- **Duplication.** There is a risk of duplication between FW-IN's engineering services and Natero IE's platform-integration services, and between FW-IN's inside sales and the European entities' field sales. Where two entities perform overlapping functions for the same benefit, the Group should be able to explain the allocation.
- **Low value-adding services.** The back-office support services provided by FW-NL and the documentation functions within FW-IN are LVA services for which a modest mark-up is appropriate. A 7.50% mark-up for FW-NL and a 15.00% mark-up for FW-IN should be tested against that standard.

## 5.2 DEMPE Analysis

Chapter VI of the OECD Guidelines requires that the allocation of profit attributable to intangibles follow the location of the functions that develop, enhance, maintain, protect, and exploit ("DEMPE") those intangibles, together with the control exercised over those functions, the assets used, and the risks borne. Legal ownership is the starting point, not the conclusion.

The Group's DEMPE allocation is set out below and summarised in the table at the end of this section.

### 5.2.1 Development

**Functions.** Substantially all development is performed by Freshworks Technologies Private Limited in India. The Group CTO states that "the vast majority of our engineering and R&D work happens in India", with development centres in Chennai and Bangalore and teams in Hyderabad, and "over 2,000 engineers in India working on our products". The CTO also confirms a "growing AI and machine learning team in India working on Freddy AI". Development activities include coding, feature building, testing, deployment, architecture, and AI model development.

Freshworks Inc. performs a limited amount of development: the CTO notes "a small number of engineers in the US who work on specific product integrations", plus product management. Product management is significant: the CPO, working with the CTO and CEO, decides "what we build and when", and "the what and the why come from the US leadership team". The Indian team retains technical autonomy on implementation — architecture choices, technology stack, coding standards.

Natero Ireland contributes specialist development in data science and platform integration; FW-NL contributes maintenance, testing, and QA support. European and APAC subsidiaries perform no product development.

**Control.** The decision on *whether* and *what* to develop is made by FW-US. The decision on *how* to implement is made in India. This split is the classic pattern in software groups with offshore engineering, and it is the crux of the DEMPE analysis: if the Indian entity merely executes specifications, it is a contract developer; if it exercises development judgement (for example, in architecture or AI model design) without bearing development risk, the arm's length return must still reflect the value of those functions even though the risk sits elsewhere.

**Assets and risk.** Development is funded entirely by FW-US, which pays FW-IN a cost-plus fee as the work is performed. FW-IN therefore bears no development risk: it recovers its costs plus a mark-up whether or not the product succeeds. FW-US bears the cost of failed development and the risk that a product does not achieve market acceptance.

**Intangible outcomes.** All IP arising from development — patents, copyright in source code, trade secrets in models and training data — is assigned to FW-US under a work-for-hire/contract R&D arrangement. The IP register confirms that "IP developed in India is assigned to and owned by the US parent".

**Assessment.** Development functions are performed in India; development risk and funding are borne in the United States; development decisions are made in the United States. The functional outcome — FW-US as the economic owner of the development intangibles, with FW-IN compensated on a cost-plus basis for execution — is defensible in principle. The remaining question is whether the cost-plus mark-up properly rewards the Indian entity for the *value* of the functions it performs, which is addressed in Section 6.4.

### 5.2.2 Enhancement

**Functions.** Ongoing product enhancement is a continuation of the development process. Releases, new features, and AI model retraining are performed by FW-IN under the same contract R&D arrangement, directed by the US product organisation. FW-NL performs maintenance, testing, and QA support functions, which support rather than drive enhancement. No subsidiary holds a separate enhancement budget or decides independently to invest in a product.

**Control and funding.** FW-US decides which enhancements to fund and in what order, and pays for them through the cost-plus fee to FW-IN as the work is performed. The Indian entity therefore does not bear the risk that an enhancement fails to deliver commercial benefit; that risk sits with FW-US.

**Economic effect.** Because FW-IN recovers its cost base plus a fixed mark-up, it does not participate in the incremental value created by the enhancements it builds. The value of enhancement accrues to FW-US as the IP owner, in the form of higher retention and expansion revenue. Net dollar retention of 108% in FY2023 is the measure of that value. This allocation is consistent with the DEMPE framework, provided that the mark-up paid to FW-IN is arm's length for the enhancement functions it performs — the point examined at Section 6.4.3.

**AI-specific enhancement.** Management notes that "AI is increasing the value of the centrally-held IP" and that AI development happens in India "under the same contract R&D arrangement". The enhancement value created by Freddy AI therefore accrues entirely to FW-US, while the engineering cost is recovered in India on a cost-plus basis. This is coherent, but it makes the arm's length level of the Indian mark-up more important, not less, because the Indian organisation is the source of an increasing share of the Group's intangible value.

### 5.2.3 Maintenance and Protection

**Maintenance functions.** Maintenance of the platform — bug fixes, security patches, infrastructure upkeep, and release management — is split between FW-IN (engineering maintenance) and FW-US (infrastructure, operations, and service-level accountability). FW-NL provides maintenance, testing, and QA support. Substantially all maintenance cost is recovered through the FW-IN cost-plus fee.

**Protection functions.** Protection of the Group's IP is performed centrally by FW-US. The CEO describes FW-US as handling "IP management and protection"; the Group legal department maintains the patent and trademark portfolios, prosecutes the 16 pending applications, renews registrations, and manages enforcement. Senior IP, legal and compliance leadership sits in the United States. FW-IN contributes indirectly through confidentiality of source code and security controls in the development environment, and trade secrets are protected by confidentiality arrangements and by employee invention assignment to FW-US. No subsidiary registers, prosecutes, or enforces any Group IP right; trademarks are "registered in the United States and licensed to subsidiaries for use in their respective territories".

**Cost.** Legal fees, prosecution costs, registration renewals, and enforcement costs are borne by FW-US and are not recharged to any subsidiary.

**Brand maintenance.** Brand equity is maintained by marketing spend. Substantially all strategic marketing spend and brand investment is incurred by FW-US; local entities run regional campaigns that support but do not create the brand. This supports the conclusion that the brand intangible is owned and maintained by FW-US.

**Assessment.** Maintenance is split between India and the United States; protection is a Parent function. Both support the conclusion that FW-US controls the preservation of the Group's intangible value, and neither is separately remunerated. The absence of a recharge for protection costs is consistent with FW-US being the residual profit claimant.

### 5.2.4 Exploitation

**Functions.** Exploitation of the Group's IP — deciding which markets to enter, how to price, which products to sell, which partners to appoint, and how to license — is controlled by FW-US. As the CRO and CEO confirm, pricing is set centrally, the product is "the same globally", and "the what and the why come from the US leadership team". The Group's European and APAC entities execute the exploitation strategy locally: they run campaigns, manage the sales cycle, and provide front-line support, "the local face of Freshworks" following a global playbook.

**Revenue and risk.** Third-party revenue is recognised by FW-US. Market risk — the risk that demand falls, that customers churn, that a product fails in a particular market or that pricing must be cut — is borne by FW-US. The COO & CFO states expressly that "if a product doesn't succeed in the German market, the loss falls on the US parent who invested in building it, not on GmbH". The Group also bears credit risk centrally, subject to the limited local credit risk management asserts.

**Remuneration of local exploitation.** The European entities are remunerated on a cost-plus basis for their sales and marketing services (FW-UK 7.07%, FW-DE 7.86%, FW-FR 8.00%, FW-NL 7.50% in FY2023). They therefore earn a service margin and do not participate in the residual profit from the markets they serve. FW-IN receives a 15.00% mark-up.

**The royalty question.** Management states that "the European entities pay us for the IP licence on a percentage-of-revenue basis" and that "the IP royalty rates are set based on external benchmarking of comparable licence arrangements". No such royalty appears in the Group's intercompany ledger for FY2021, FY2022 or FY2023, no IP licence agreement was provided, and no royalty benchmark study is on file. This is the most consequential open item in this Master File (Section 10, item 3), because it determines whether a material controlled transaction — the licence of Group IP to the entities that exploit it — has been priced, documented and reported at all. Two scenarios follow, and both require action:

- **If a royalty is charged**, it is a material controlled transaction that must be quantified, benchmarked (typically by reference to comparable licence arrangements or a residual profit split), documented in an IP licence agreement, and reflected in the subsidiaries' statutory accounts and tax returns.
- **If no royalty is charged**, then the subsidiaries exploit Group IP without consideration. That is defensible only if their remuneration as service providers already captures the value of their functions; but it must be positively documented and consistently maintained. It is not consistent with management's description of the arrangements, and the inconsistency itself is a documentation risk.

# 6. Transfer Pricing Policy Framework

## 6.1 Policy Overview

### 6.1.1 Principles

The Group's transfer pricing policy is set centrally by Freshworks Inc. with input from external advisors, and is applied uniformly across the Group. The policy rests on four principles:

1. **Single principal, limited-risk subsidiaries.** Freshworks Inc. is the entrepreneurial principal. It owns all intellectual property, funds all expenditure, contracts with customers, and bears all material risks. Each subsidiary performs a defined, routine or supporting function and earns a return commensurate with that function.
2. **Cost-plus for services.** Subsidiaries that provide services to Freshworks Inc. are remunerated on a full cost mark-up ("FCMU") basis. The mark-up is set by reference to benchmark studies prepared periodically.
3. **Annual review.** Transfer prices are reviewed annually as part of the year-end close. Where the cost base, business model, or market conditions change materially, the prices are adjusted. Benchmarking studies are refreshed every two to three years.
4. **Contemporaneous documentation.** This Master File and the accompanying Local Files are prepared annually to document the policy, the transactions, and the arm's length nature of the results.

### 6.1.2 Method Selection

The Group applies the transactional net margin method ("TNMM") with FCMU as the profit level indicator for all of its service transactions. The choice is appropriate for the transactions in question:

- The subsidiaries are limited-risk service providers with no intangibles and no unique contributions. TNMM is the method OECD Chapter II recommends where one party to a transaction is the least complex and does not make unique contributions.
- Reliable internal or external CUPs are not available: the services are bespoke, performed within a vertically integrated group, and not traded between independent parties in a comparable form.
- RPM would be inappropriate because no subsidiary resells to third parties under the evidenced arrangements (see Section 4.2.1).
- PSM would be inappropriate because no subsidiary makes a unique contribution to the combined profit; the profit split would reduce to allocating residual profit to FW-US, which TNMM with a cost-plus tested party achieves more simply.
- CPM would be a reasonable alternative, but TNMM/FCMU is used because FCMU is computable from published financial data for a wide comparable set and, mathematically, FCMU applied to a full cost base is equivalent to CPM where the cost base is the same.

### 6.1.3 The Arm's Length Range

The Group uses the interquartile range ("IQR") of the comparable distribution as its arm's length range. This is consistent with OECD Guidelines paragraph 3.62 and with the approach adopted in each of the Group's benchmark studies. Where a tested party's result falls within the IQR, no adjustment is made. Where it falls outside the IQR, an adjustment to the nearest bound (or to the median, where the result exceeds the upper bound, consistent with paragraph 3.62) is justified.

### 6.1.4 Policy Coherence

Two matters of policy coherence should be recorded at the outset, because they affect how the whole framework should be read:

- **The Group's own methodology documentation is inconsistent with its benchmark studies.** The Group's intercompany ledger methodology tab states a mark-up range of 12–18% for contract R&D services. No benchmark study in the documentation pack supports a 12–18% range for contract software development or contract R&D: the highest upper bound across the four software-development and contract-R&D studies is 12.49% (Software Development Services — Europe), and the study covering the actual tested party (Asia-Pacific) has an upper bound of 10.37%. The 12–18% range appears to be an unsupported policy statement and must be replaced (Section 10, item 1).
- **Several benchmark studies have no tested party.** The Software Development Services (Americas), Software Development Services (Europe) and Contract R&D Services (Americas) studies state "None in region — study retained for regional coverage and consistency". They do not test any Group entity. They are useful as context but cannot support any actual pricing outcome, and they should not be cited as support for a pricing position.

## 6.4 Policies for Services

### 6.4.1 Policy

All intra-group services are remunerated on a cost-plus basis using TNMM with FCMU as the profit level indicator. The mechanism is uniform: the service provider's operating cost base (principally personnel costs plus local overheads, depreciation, and occupancy) is identified, a mark-up is applied, and the resulting amount is invoiced to Freshworks Inc. quarterly. The mark-up rate is reviewed annually and benchmarked periodically.

The policy has one important feature and one important limitation. The **feature** is that it removes all profit volatility from the subsidiaries: they recover costs plus a fixed return regardless of the Group's commercial performance, consistent with their limited-risk characterisation. The **limitation** is that a uniform mechanism is only arm's length if the mark-up applied to each cost base is itself within the arm's length range for the functions being performed. Applying a cost-plus formula is not, by itself, evidence of compliance.

### 6.4.3 FW-IN: The Central Issue

**The 15.00% mark-up applied to Freshworks Technologies Private Limited in FY2023 lies outside the arm's length range established by the Group's own benchmark study.** The Software Development Services (Asia-Pacific) study identifies FW-IN as its tested party, uses 15 independent Asia-Pacific comparables, and concludes an IQR of 8.34% to 10.37% with a median of 9.32%. The study is explicit about the consequence: "Should the actual margin exceed 10.37%, a downward adjustment would be justified, typically to the median of 9.32%."

The financial effect is material. FW-IN's FY2023 cost base, derived from the invoiced fee of US$258,271,047 at a 15.00% mark-up, is approximately US$224.6 million. The mark-up earned is therefore approximately US$33.7 million. Applying the benchmarked range:

| Measure | Mark-up | FW-IN Revenue (US$) | Difference from actual (US$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Actual | 15.00% | 258,271,047 | — |
| Q3 (upper bound) | 10.37% | 247,872,830 | (10,398,217) |
| Median | 9.32% | 245,514,703 | (12,756,344) |
| Q1 (lower bound) | 8.34% | 243,313,260 | (14,957,787) |

A downward adjustment to the median would reduce FW-IN's FY2023 revenue, and increase Freshworks Inc.'s taxable profit in the United States, by approximately **US$12.8 million**. At the upper bound the adjustment is approximately US$10.4 million. These amounts are annual and recurring: the same 15.00% rate was applied in FY2021 and FY2022, so the cumulative exposure across the three-year period is of the order of US$30–40 million, before interest and penalties.

The exposure is not one-sided. Because the adjustment reduces Indian taxable profit and increases US taxable profit, an Indian transfer pricing audit would be expected to make the same downward adjustment under section 92 of the Income Tax Act (Indian authorities routinely require an *increase* in the Indian entity's income, and would resist a reduction unless the taxpayer itself claims it). The Group's position is therefore exposed to a primary adjustment in India even though the correct arm's length outcome points the other way. This is precisely the scenario in which a mutual agreement procedure or an APA would be valuable.

**Why the 15.00% rate appears to have been adopted.** The Group's ledger methodology tab states that the contract R&D mark-up range is 12–18%. That range has no support in the benchmark studies on file. The most likely explanation is that the rate was set by reference to Indian comparables at a different point in time, or reflecting the fact that FW-IN performs functions beyond routine development — it also runs global customer support, inside sales, and back-office operations, and its engineers work on Freddy AI, which the study's comparables would have been screened out for ("no material IP development"). Those considerations may justify a higher return than the comparable set suggests, but they do not justify a return outside the range that the Group's own study establishes for that tested party. If the Group believes the study's comparable set is not representative of FW-IN's actual functional profile, the correct response is to re-run the study with an appropriate comparable set — not to apply a rate that the study contradicts.

### 6.4.4 Natero IE: A Minor but Unjustified Variance

Natero Ireland Limited's 5.00% mark-up is below the lower bound of the Contract R&D Services (Europe) study (Q1 8.58%, median 9.67%). The absolute amounts are small — on a FY2023 cost base of approximately €391,562, a movement from 5.00% to 9.67% would increase revenue by roughly €18,000 — so the item is not material in any jurisdiction. It is nonetheless a departure from policy and should be corrected, because an out-of-range result in a small entity is as much a documentation failure as an out-of-range result in a large one, and because the correction is trivially cheap.

### 6.4.5 Management and Shared Services

The Group's benchmark studies include three Management Services studies (Americas, Europe, Asia-Pacific). Their existence presupposes that management and shared services are provided by Freshworks Inc. to subsidiaries on a cost-plus basis, and management confirms this: "The management and shared services fees are also cost-plus, where we allocate the costs of the central functions and add an appropriate margin."

No such fees appear in the intercompany ledger for FY2021 to FY2023. The Management Services studies therefore support a transaction that is not recorded. Two consequences follow:

1. If management fees are charged, they are missing from the ledger and from this documentation, and must be added.
2. If they are not charged, the Group must document why: central services such as IT infrastructure, HR policy, legal, finance, and treasury are provided from the United States, and a subsidiary that receives them without charge is receiving a benefit for which an independent enterprise would pay. The studies analysed above establish that the charge can be priced (Europe IQR 7.05%–10.76%; Asia-Pacific 5.41%–10.65%); the Group needs to establish whether it is being made.

The Group should also confirm that no shareholder or stewardship costs — SEC reporting, investor relations, board costs, group audit fees, listing compliance, group-level M&A advisory — are included in any management fee cost base. These are not chargeable to subsidiaries under Chapter VII of the OECD Guidelines.

### 6.4.6 Double Testing of FW-NL

FW-NL appears as a tested party both in the Sales & Marketing Services (Europe) study and in the Management Services (Europe) study. Its FY2023 mark-up of 7.50% is tested above against Management Services (Europe). Audit practice would expect the Group to identify which study governs FW-NL's actual activity — its statutory accounts describe software maintenance, testing and QA support, back-office IT support, consultancy and advisory support, documentation, and customer and market research support, which is closer to the Management Services scope than to field sales — and to apply a single, consistent benchmark. Testing the same entity against two different ranges invites the criticism that the Group selects whichever range is convenient.

## 6.5 Policies for Intangible Property

### 6.5.1 Policy as Described

The Group's stated policy is that Freshworks Inc. owns all Group IP and licenses it to the operating subsidiaries. Management describes the arrangement as follows: "The European entities pay us for the IP licence on a percentage-of-revenue basis"; "The IP royalty rates are set based on external benchmarking of comparable licence arrangements." The IP register confirms that trademarks are "licensed to subsidiaries for use in their respective territories".

### 6.5.2 Policy as Applied

**No royalty is recorded.** The Group's intercompany ledger for FY2021 to FY2023 contains no royalty or IP licence fees in any direction. No royalties appear in the statutory accounts of FW-UK, FW-DE, FW-FR, FW-NL, Natero IE or FW-IN: the accounts show operating costs comprising personnel, occupancy, and administrative costs only, with no licence line. No IP licence agreement was provided. No royalty benchmark study is on file.

The position is, in other words, described but not evidenced. That is the single largest documentation deficiency in this Master File.

### 6.5.3 Analysis

There are two internally coherent positions available to the Group. Each is defensible; neither is currently documented.

**Position A: no royalty is payable, because the subsidiaries do not exploit Group IP for their own account.** On this analysis the subsidiaries are pure service providers. FW-UK, FW-DE and FW-FR do not buy and resell subscriptions; they perform sales and marketing services for FW-US, which is the principal. They do not acquire a licence to exploit the software; they market a product that Freshworks Inc. sells. Their remuneration — a cost-plus service fee — compensates them for their functions, and no separate consideration for IP is necessary because they derive no IP-related profit. This is the position that the audited accounts support. To sustain it, the Group must (i) maintain the service-provider characterisation consistently (see Section 4.3.3 and Section 10, item 4), (ii) ensure the cost-plus mark-ups are within range (Section 6.4), and (iii) confirm that local entities do not hold customer contracts or other intangibles (Section 4.2.1).

**Position B: a royalty is payable, because the subsidiaries use Group IP and brand to generate local revenue.** On this analysis the subsidiaries operate under the Freshworks brand, use centrally developed marketing collateral and sales tools, and localise content; the brand and the product are the substance of what they sell. If the subsidiaries are characterised as limited-risk distributors — as management describes them — they would ordinarily acquire product distribution rights for a royalty or pay a distributor margin that reflects the value of the IP. To sustain this position, the Group must (i) establish the royalty base (net or gross revenue) and rate, (ii) benchmark it by reference to comparable uncontrolled licence arrangements or a profit split, (iii) execute a licence agreement, and (iv) record the royalty in the ledger and in each entity's accounts.

The Group cannot adopt both positions. Its documentation currently describes Position B while its accounts and ledger reflect Position A. That combination is the worst of both worlds: it incurs the disclosure risk of a royalty arrangement without the pricing and documentation that would support it, and it leaves the cost-plus results exposed to challenge on the ground that the subsidiaries' returns have not been tested net of an imputed royalty.

### 6.5.4 The Assignment of IP Developed in India

A related and separate matter concerns the assignment of IP developed by FW-IN to Freshworks Inc. The IP register states that "All IP developed by Freshworks Technologies Private Limited (India) is assigned to Freshworks Inc. (US) under the contract R&D services agreement", and that India develops "under a work-for-hire arrangement, so anything they create belongs to us".

In principle, a contract R&D arrangement in which the developer receives an arm's length cost-plus fee — including an appropriate mark-up for its development functions — adequately compensates the developer for the IP it creates, and no separate assignment payment is required. That is the Group's position and it is a defensible one, provided the mark-up is arm's length. It is precisely here that the 15.00% mark-up matters most: if the mark-up is above the arm's length range, the Group is paying India more than the benchmark supports; if the Group instead argued that no separate consideration was paid for the IP assignment, the Indian authorities could counter that the IP created in India (including the patents and the Freddy AI model) was transferred for nil consideration and should have been compensated. The Group should be prepared to explain, in the Indian Local File, why the cost-plus fee is full consideration for the IP assignment.

### 6.5.5 Recommended Action

The Group should decide, at Board level and before the FY2024 documentation cycle, whether it operates Position A or Position B, and then document the chosen position consistently across this Master File, the Local Files, the intercompany agreements, the ledger, and the statutory accounts. If Position B is chosen, a royalty benchmark study must be commissioned. If Position A is chosen, the management statements describing a percentage-of-revenue royalty and a benchmarked royalty rate should be corrected in the functional analysis record so that they do not appear in a future audit as admissions against the Group's own position.

## 6.6 Policies for Financial Transactions

### 6.6.1 Policy as Described

Management describes an intercompany financing policy under which the US parent provides funding to subsidiaries through intercompany loans: "We have formal loan agreements in place. The interest rate is set based on a reference rate — typically the applicable base rate for the currency involved — plus a spread that reflects the creditworthiness of the borrowing entity. For our European subsidiaries, that spread has typically been in the range of 100 to 200 basis points over the base rate. The loans have defined repayment schedules and are reviewed annually."

Management also confirms that subsidiaries do not raise external debt or equity independently, that the Group has approximately US$1.07 billion in cash and investments, that it has no external debt, that it manages currency exposure centrally, and that it hedges INR exposure on the Indian cost base with FX forward contracts of approximately US$50 million notional. Management confirms there is no formal cash pooling arrangement.

### 6.6.2 Policy as Applied

**The Group's intercompany loan register records no formal loan facility at any point in FY2021, FY2022 or FY2023.** The register states that no intercompany loan was in place at 31 December 2023 and that no interest was accrued or paid in any of the three years. The intercompany ledger contains no interest line.

This is a direct contradiction between the interview record and the Group's financial records. The register is the more reliable evidence and is adopted here: **no intercompany loans existed during FY2023, and consequently no interest was charged or paid.** The contradiction should nonetheless be resolved, because the same interview record supplies the functional analysis on which the cost-plus characterisations rest, and an unexplained inconsistency in one area weakens confidence in the others (Section 10, item 6).

### 6.6.3 Analysis

If, as the register indicates, subsidiaries are funded through trading balances rather than loans, the financing question does not disappear. A subsidiary that invoices Freshworks Inc. quarterly and is paid in arrears is extending trade credit to its Parent. Where that credit is prolonged, it is economically equivalent to a loan and an arm's length interest charge would be expected. The Group's intercompany receivables are modest — FW-UK's £4.18 million receivable at 31 December 2023 represents roughly six to seven weeks of turnover — which is consistent with ordinary trade terms and does not, on its face, indicate Parent funding of subsidiaries or subsidiary funding of the Parent on a scale that would require an interest imputation.

The Group's centralised treasury function, its US$1.07 billion cash balance, and its absence of external debt mean that the Group as a whole is not balance-sheet constrained. A subsidiary that requires working capital is funded by its Parent's trading terms rather than by borrowings, and a subsidiary that accumulates cash holds it locally rather than depositing it with a Group treasury vehicle. That is a simple structure and reduces transfer pricing risk; it also means the Group has no cash-pooling, guarantee, or bonding transactions to document.

### 6.6.4 Recommended Position

1. **Confirm nil.** Retain a contemporaneous statement in each Local File confirming that no intercompany loan, guarantee, cash pool, or other financing transaction existed during FY2023, supported by the loan register.
2. **Correct the functional analysis record.** The interview statements describing loan agreements, spreads of 100 to 200 basis points, and defined repayment schedules should be annotated as not reflecting the Group's actual position, so that they are not relied on by an auditor as evidence of transactions the Group did not undertake.
3. **Monitor trade balances.** Establish a policy for intercompany payment terms (for example, 30 or 45 days) and monitor days-sales-outstanding on intercompany receivables. If a balance exceeds the policy term materially and for a sustained period, an arm's length interest charge should be considered.
4. **If a loan is later required** — for example, to fund a new market entry — it should be priced by reference to a benchmark of comparable third-party borrowings reflecting the borrower's creditworthiness and the currency of the loan, documented in a written agreement, and supported by a benchmarking study. The Group currently has no such study.

### 7.1.1 Capital Adequacy of Subsidiaries

A transfer pricing question arises whenever a subsidiary is thinly capitalised relative to the functions it performs, because an independent enterprise performing those functions would require adequate working capital and would either hold more equity or pay for debt funding. On the evidence available, the subsidiaries' equity balances are small but their working capital needs are modest (they are paid in arrears, hold no inventory, and employ limited numbers of staff), and no subsidiary's balance sheet shows a funding shortfall that would require an imputed interest deduction or a capital adjustment. The Group should nonetheless monitor this annually in each Local File, particularly for the entities with the largest cost bases.

## 7.2 External Funding Arrangements

**The Group has no external funding arrangements.** At 31 December 2023 there were no syndicated loans, term loans, bond issuances, revolving credit facilities, convertible notes, or other external borrowings at any entity within the Group. There are no external finance leases of significance, and the Group's operating lease liabilities (US$26.8 million non-current at 31 December 2023) arise from office leases and are not financing transactions in the transfer pricing sense.

The Group's liquidity position is strong: net cash provided by operating activities was US$86.2 million in FY2023, and the Group holds substantial cash and investments with no debt. Management confirms that the Group holds approximately US$1.07 billion in cash and investments and that subsidiaries "don't raise external debt or equity independently".

**Consequences for transfer pricing.** Because there is no external debt, there is no external interest cost to allocate, no need for a treasury centre or financing company, and no group-level borrowing to be recharged. The Group consequently has no transactions of the kinds addressed by Chapter X of the OECD Guidelines beyond the potential for intercompany lending discussed at Section 7.3. There are no external lenders whose creditworthiness or covenants would need to be considered in assessing whether an intercompany arrangement is arm's length.

## 7.4 Cash Management and Pooling

**There is no cash pooling arrangement, physical or notional, and no intercompany netting agreement.** Management confirms: "Not formally. Each entity maintains its own bank accounts and manages its own day-to-day cash. The US treasury function monitors balances across the group and will provide or recall funding through the intercompany loan mechanism as needed. We have considered implementing a formal cash pool but have not done so yet."

Treasury operations are managed centrally from the United States by the Group CFO and the VP Tax & Treasury, working with the Director of Corporate Finance. The treasury function monitors Group liquidity, manages the investment portfolio, and manages foreign exchange exposure. It does not operate a treasury centre entity: there is no Group finance company, no in-house bank, and no entity whose business is to provide financing to affiliates.

**Foreign exchange management.** The Group manages currency exposure centrally. It hedges INR exposure arising on the Indian cost base with foreign exchange forward contracts of approximately US$50 million notional. Because FX contracts are entered into by Freshworks Inc. and hedge a cost exposure that the Parent bears (the INR cost base is paid through the USD-denominated cost-plus fee), the hedging gains and losses accrue to the Parent.

**Foreign exchange risk in the subsidiaries.** The European subsidiaries invoice in their local currency and their cost bases are largely local, so their FX exposure is limited to the mismatch between local-currency receipts and any USD-denominated elements. Management identifies "some foreign exchange risk on their local currency revenues versus USD-denominated intercompany charges"; because the subsidiaries' revenue is itself a local-currency cost-plus fee, the exposure is modest and is a normal incident of the service-provider function rather than a separately remunerated risk.

**Consequences for transfer pricing.** The absence of pooling, netting, and treasury-centre activity materially simplifies the Group's position: there is no Chapter X cash-pooling transaction to document, no allocation of pooling benefits to be justified, and no treasury centre whose remuneration would need to reflect the value of its functions.

## 7.5 Guarantees and Credit Support

**The Group has no intercompany guarantee, bond, letter of comfort, or other credit support arrangement.** No Group entity has guaranteed the obligations of another; no parent guarantee supports subsidiary borrowings (because there are none); and no subsidiary has provided an upstream or cross-stream guarantee.

Because there are no guarantees, there is no guarantee fee to price and no guarantee-fee methodology to document. This is consistent with the Group's capital structure: with no external debt at any entity, there is nothing for a guarantee to support.

Two related matters should be noted:

1. **Customer credit support.** The Group does not provide credit support to customers and does not extend credit in the lending sense; customers pay subscription fees in advance. Credit risk is limited to collection of trade receivables, which is borne principally by Freshworks Inc. as the party to the customer contracts (subject to the unresolved question at Section 4.2.1 about whether local entities hold contracts and bear local credit risk).
2. **Letters of comfort.** No letters of comfort or support have been issued to third parties in respect of any Group entity. If any exist, they should be disclosed and assessed under Chapter X of the OECD Guidelines, because a letter of comfort can constitute implicit credit support with a transfer pricing consequence.

**Figure 3 — Treasury and Funding Structure (narrative description in place of diagram).** Funds flow from third-party customers to Freshworks Inc. as the contracting party; Freshworks Inc. pays cost-plus service fees to FW-IN, FW-UK, FW-DE, FW-FR, FW-NL and Natero IE; those entities pay their local staff, landlords, and suppliers. There is no external borrowing, no intercompany lending, no cash pooling, and no guarantee. The only funding relationship between entities is the trade receivable created by the service fees, settled on normal commercial terms.

1. **FW-IN's fiscal year.** FW-IN closes on 31 March. 6. **The mark-up is not always reproducible from the accounts.** FW-FR's "charges d'exploitation" of €6,424,337 include items offset by "reprises" of €1,112,508, so a simple ratio of operating result to operating expenses does not reproduce the 8.00% contractual mark-up exactly. The Group should maintain a transfer pricing computation schedule for each entity, reconciled to the audited accounts, as part of annual documentation.

### 8.2.6 The Profit Outcome

Every subsidiary earns a modest, stable profit and the Parent absorbs the entire loss. FW-IN is the Group's only consistently profitable operating entity (net profit ~US$24.9m at 31 March 2024); the European entities earn between 5.4% and 7.4% net margins; the Parent records a consolidated operating loss of US$170.2 million and carries all stock-based compensation.

That pattern is the expected outcome of a cost-plus policy applied by a loss-making principal, and it is defensible where the Parent genuinely bears the risks and performs the functions described in Sections 4 and 5. It is not defensible where the mark-ups themselves are outside the arm's length range, which is the case for FW-IN (Section 6.4.3) and Natero IE (Section 6.4.4). In substance the structure transfers a fixed return to India and Europe and leaves the volatile residual in the United States; the arm's length question is whether the fixed returns are correctly sized, not whether the structure as a whole is appropriate.

## 10.3 The Three Most Material Items

### 10.3.1 Item 3 — The Unrecorded Royalty (and Management Fee)

This is the item that most affects the credibility of the Group's position. The Group's own management describes a royalty arrangement that the Group's own accounts do not show. The consequences are set out at Sections 5.2.4 and 6.5: either the Group is operating a material controlled transaction that has never been priced, benchmarked, documented or reported, or it is not operating it and its functional analysis record is wrong. There is no third possibility, and the current documentation supports neither position.

The Group should resolve this before the FY2024 documentation cycle. If a royalty is to be introduced, the Group should consider the effect on the subsidiaries' cost-plus returns: a distributor or licensee that pays a royalty must earn a return sufficient to cover it, and the existing mark-ups of 7.07% to 8.00% may not survive the deduction of a royalty from the same operating margin.

### 10.3.2 Item 4 — Characterisation

The Group uses three descriptions of the same entities: cost-plus service providers (the accounts), limited-risk distributors (management), and entities that hold customer contracts and bear local credit risk (management). Only the first is supported by evidence. Because the characterisation determines the method, the tested party, the comparables, and the mark-up, the Group cannot sustain a position while three descriptions coexist. The Group should settle the characterisation from the executed customer contracts and the actual conduct of the parties, and then rewrite this document's Sections 3.4, 4.2, 4.3.3, 4.5 and 6.4 to reflect it consistently.

### 10.3.3 Items 1 and 2 — The FW-IN Mark-up

This is the item with the largest quantified exposure: a 15.00% mark-up against a benchmarked range of 8.34% to 10.37%, giving an annual adjustment of approximately US$10.4 million to US$12.8 million on FY2023 volumes and a three-year exposure of the order of US$30–40 million before interest and penalties (Section 6.4.3). The exposure runs in an unusual direction — an Indian audit would ordinarily assert that the Indian entity is *under*-paid, whereas the Group's own study implies it is *over*-paid — which means the Group is exposed both to a challenge in India and to a corresponding adjustment risk in the United States. This is the strongest candidate for an APA (Section 9.1).

## C.4 Reconciliation to Consolidated Expense Categories

FW-IN's single cost-plus fee of US$258.3 million is paid by Freshworks Inc. and is recorded across several consolidated expense lines, because the Indian entity performs engineering (research and development), customer support (cost of revenue), inside sales (sales and marketing) and back-office functions (general and administrative). The Group does not document the split. For illustration, FW-IN's fee is 43.3% of consolidated revenue and is nearly twice the consolidated research and development expense of US$137.8 million, which confirms that a substantial portion of the fee is recorded outside the R&D line. The allocation should be documented (Section 10, item 2), because it determines where the Group's largest single deduction is claimed and how the Indian cost base is characterised against the benchmark studies.
